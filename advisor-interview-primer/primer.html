The Advisor Interview Primer

The concepts a hiring manager expects a new financial advisor to explain clearly: the standard of care, the planning process, portfolio basics, accounts, and how to answer the questions you will be asked.

If you remember nothing else

The 60-second version

  1. The client's interest comes first. Know the difference between a fiduciary and the best-interest standard.
  2. Plan first, then pick products. Goals and risk drive the portfolio, never the other way around.
  3. Asset allocation matters most. Diversify, rebalance, and match risk to the time horizon.
  4. Time is the biggest lever. Compounding rewards starting early. Inflation punishes sitting in cash.
  5. The job is behavioral coaching. Keeping clients invested through a downturn is where advisors earn their fee.

The role

Who you serve, the standard you're held to, and how you're paid

Fiduciary · RIA

Registered Investment Adviser

Owes a fiduciary duty under the Investment Advisers Act of 1940: loyalty and care, ongoing, across the whole relationship. Conflicts must be avoided or fully disclosed. Regulated by the SEC or the state.

Best interest · Broker-dealer

Registered Representative

Held to Regulation Best Interest (Reg BI, 2020) at the time of each recommendation. It replaced the older "suitability" standard. Typically paid by commission. Regulated by FINRA and the SEC.

Many advisors are dual-registered and wear both hats. Be ready to say which hat applies to a given recommendation, and disclose it on Form CRS.

The planning process · CFP Board, 7 steps

Every client relationship follows the same path

  1. Understandthe client's personal and financial situation
  2. Identifyand select goals
  3. Analyzethe current course and alternatives
  4. Developrecommendations
  5. Presentthe recommendations
  6. Implementthe plan
  7. Monitorprogress and update

Interview tip: when asked "how would you work with a new client?", walk through these steps in order. It shows you plan before you sell.

2:10
Know your client

Three inputs set the right amount of risk

Risk tolerance

How much volatility the client is willing to stomach. It's emotional, so measure it with a questionnaire and a conversation.

Risk capacity

How much loss the client can afford: income, net worth, emergency fund, debts, dependents. It's financial.

Time horizon

When the money is needed. Longer horizons can recover from drawdowns. Money needed within 1–3 years belongs in safer assets.

When tolerance and capacity disagree, the lower one wins. An aggressive 62-year-old with no savings cushion still gets a conservative plan.

2:30
Portfolio basics

More expected return always costs more volatility

Asset allocationThe stock/bond/cash mix explains most of the variation in a portfolio's returns over time (Brinson studies, ~90%).
DiversificationOwning assets that don't move together lowers risk without giving up as much return. It reduces risk but doesn't remove it.
RebalancingTrim what grew, add to what lagged, and get back to the target mix. This forces "buy low, sell high."
Rates ↑ = bond prices ↓Bond prices move opposite to interest rates. Longer duration means bigger price swings.
3:00
Time value of money

Ten extra years roughly doubles the result

Rule of 7272 ÷ return rate = years to double. At 8%, money doubles in about 9 years.
InflationAt 3% inflation, prices double in about 24 years. "Safe" cash quietly loses buying power.
Dollar-cost averagingInvest a fixed amount on a schedule. It removes the need to time the market and builds the habit.

3:50
Beyond investments

A plan covers four more areas

Retirement income

  • 4% rule: a starting withdrawal rate that historically lasted 30 years
  • Social Security full retirement age: 67 (born 1960+)
  • RMDs begin at 73
  • Sequence risk: a crash early in retirement hurts most

Risk management

  • Term life: cheap, temporary, pure protection
  • Permanent (whole, universal): lifelong coverage plus cash value, costs more
  • Disability insurance protects the biggest asset, which is future income
  • Emergency fund: 3–6 months of expenses

Tax planning

  • Asset location: hold tax-inefficient assets in tax-advantaged accounts
  • Tax-loss harvesting offsets gains
  • Roth conversions in low-income years

Estate planning

  • Will, power of attorney, healthcare directive
  • Beneficiary designations override a will, so review them yearly
  • Trusts can avoid probate and control distribution

Behavior & compliance

Clients' biggest risk is usually their own reactions

Loss aversionLosses feel about twice as painful as equal gains feel good.
Recency biasAssuming the last few months will continue forever.
Herd behaviorBuying what everyone is buying, usually near the top.
AnchoringFixating on a purchase price or an old account high.
Never say or do
  • Guarantee a return, or call any investment "risk-free"
  • Recommend a product before you understand the client's goals and risk profile
  • Skip documentation. If it isn't written down, it didn't happen.

Likely questions · answer frames

What they'll ask, and how to structure your answer

"Why do you want to be a financial advisor?"

STORY → SERVICE → GROWTHA real moment money mattered to you or your family, why helping people plan motivates you, and why you want to build a long career here.

"The market drops 20%. A client calls to sell everything. What do you do?"

LISTEN → RE-ANCHOR → DOCUMENTAcknowledge the fear first. Go back to their goals and time horizon, and remind them that selling locks in the loss. If they still want to act, respect it and document it. Never promise a recovery date.

"Explain a Roth IRA like I'm 20 years old."

ANALOGY → ONE NUMBER → CHECK"Pay the tax on the seed, not the harvest." You pay tax now, and everything it grows into comes out tax-free in retirement. Then ask: "Does that make sense?"

"How will you find clients?"

NETWORK → NICHE → PARTNERS → ACTIVITYYour natural market, a niche you understand (e.g. teachers, young families), referrals from CPAs and attorneys, and a daily activity goal you track, such as calls and meetings per week.

"Tell me about a time you faced rejection or failure."

STAR: SITUATION → TASK → ACTION → RESULTA real example where you heard "no" and kept going. End with what you learned. Advisors hear "no" daily, so they're testing resilience.

"What's the difference between a fiduciary and a broker?"

DEFINE → CONTRAST → WHY IT MATTERSFiduciary duty is ongoing, under the Advisers Act. Reg BI applies at each recommendation. Then say you would put the client first whichever hat you're wearing.

Before you walk in

Day-of checklist

Look up this morning

  • Where the S&P 500 and Dow closed yesterday
  • The current Fed funds rate and 10-year Treasury yield
  • One market headline you can discuss in two sentences
  • The firm's clients, fee model, and whether it's an RIA, a broker-dealer, or both

Questions to ask them

  • What does training and licensing sponsorship look like in the first 12 months?
  • How are new advisors paid during the ramp-up period?
  • Where do clients come from: firm leads or my own book?
  • What does success look like at 90 days and at one year?
